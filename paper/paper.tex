\title{\textbf{How Well Does a Kepler-Trained Transit Classifier Transfer to
TESS? A Characterization Study}}
\author{Dhruv Ramani}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Machine-learning studies of transiting-exoplanet detection almost always report
a single in-mission accuracy or ROC-AUC. We argue this is the wrong figure of
merit and present instead a \emph{characterized decision system}: an
interpretable, physics-informed pipeline (Transit Least Squares $\rightarrow$
eight physical features $\rightarrow$ a calibrated RandomForest\,+\,XGBoost
ensemble $\rightarrow$ five orthogonal physics-vetting checks) whose behavior we
map, and validate, rather than summarize. On a $500$-target labeled TESS
benchmark: (i) \textbf{injection--recovery} maps detection completeness across
period and radius, revealing a ``sweet spot'' bounded below by the noise floor,
above by eclipsing-binary rejection, and toward long periods by transits per
sector. (ii) \textbf{Cross-mission transfer} is severe but real: an ensemble
scoring ROC-AUC $0.96$ in Kepler cross-validation falls to $0.72$ (95\% CI
$[0.67,0.76]$) on TESS, with the Brier score degrading from $0.08$ to $0.29$; the
separating direction of the depth and radius features \emph{inverts} between
missions, a concrete mechanism for the gap. The ensemble nonetheless remains the
best cross-mission model, beating a one-parameter SNR cut and every other learner.
(iii) \textbf{The operating point matters more than the model}: because the
transferred probabilities are compressed, the conventional $p\geq0.5$ cut recovers
only $28\%$ of true planets and is entirely blind to two subgroups --- every
planet with $P<2$\,d and every one deeper than $20{,}000$\,ppm, precisely TESS's
most characteristic detections. A cross-validated threshold of $0.117$ (identical
in every fold) raises recall to $0.72$ and $F_1$ from $0.41$ to $0.70$, while
retraining without the inverting features does not help at all. (iv)
\textbf{ML $\times$ physics}: the two independent verdicts \emph{agree}
informatively --- both-agree-planet is $79\%$ pure ($[0.68,0.87]$, $3.7\times$
enriched) and physics vetting independently rejects $54\%$ of false positives ---
but we test and report an honest negative: their \emph{disagreement} does not
robustly identify extra planets, and a physics-informed follow-up ranking shows no
reliable advantage over probability alone at this sample size. Every claim carries
a bootstrap or permutation-based interval, and the whole study reproduces
end-to-end from public archives.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The transiting-exoplanet literature has converged on supervised classifiers that
map light curves or hand-built features to a planet/false-positive label, and
report their success as an accuracy or ROC-AUC on a held-out set drawn from the
\emph{same} mission \cite{shallue2018,exominer2021}. Such a number answers
``how separable is my test set?'' but not the questions a survey actually asks:
\emph{what can this pipeline detect}, \emph{does it work on data it was not
trained on}, and \emph{where should a human look?} Recent work adds probability
calibration and multimodal inputs \cite{exonet2026} but still frames the output
as a single vetting score.

We take a different stance: the output of a detection pipeline should be a
\emph{characterized decision}, and every claim about it should carry an
uncertainty and survive a validation check. Our pipeline emits two independent
judgements --- a calibrated ML probability and an interpretable five-check physics
verdict --- and we ask not only how well each works but where they agree, where
they disagree, and whether that structure is actionable. Our contributions are:
(1) an injection--recovery completeness map for a feature-based classical ensemble
on real TESS data; (2) an honest, confidence-interval-qualified account of
Kepler$\rightarrow$TESS transfer, with a concrete distribution-shift mechanism;
(3) the finding that the \emph{decision threshold}, not the model, dominates the
practical cost of that shift --- with two subgroups rendered wholly invisible by
the conventional cut, and two model-level remedies shown not to help;
(4) an analysis of the ML--physics agreement structure, including a validated
positive result (agreement is strongly predictive; physics adds orthogonal
false-positive rejection) and an honest negative one (disagreement does not
robustly surface extra planets, and physics-informed triage shows no reliable
gain); and (4) a validation battery --- permutation test, association tests, and a
catalog cross-check --- confirming the results are not artifacts. Every figure
and number regenerates end-to-end from public archives.

A note on scope: we deliberately report what does \emph{not} replicate. Two
tentative effects visible at a $160$-target sample --- a physical SNR cut beating
the learned models, and a follow-up-efficiency advantage for disagreement-aware
ranking --- vanished when we grew the benchmark to $500$. We keep them in as a
cautionary result about over-reading small-sample structure, which is itself part
of the paper's point.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Training (Kepler).} We train on the NASA Exoplanet Archive KOI cumulative
catalog, using the definitive dispositions \textsc{confirmed} (positive) and
\textsc{false positive} (negative) and excluding \textsc{candidate}s
($N=7325$; 2745 planets, 4580 false positives).

\textbf{Test (TESS).} We build a labeled TESS benchmark from ExoFOP TFOPWG
dispositions --- \textsc{cp}/\textsc{kp} as planets, \textsc{fp}/\textsc{fa} as
false positives --- and run the full pipeline on a class-balanced sample of $500$
TIC targets ($250/250$). This is the only place true TESS labels enter, and it
measures real deployment performance. The dispositioned-TOI base rate is itself
$\approx\!0.50$ (1333 planets vs.\ 1349 false positives on ExoFOP), so the balanced
benchmark matches deployment prevalence for the vetting task; we additionally
report Bayes-adjusted purities for rarer, blind-search prevalences.

\textbf{Injection corpus.} For completeness testing we inject synthetic transits
into real TESS light curves screened to be transit-free (pre-injection TLS
SDE $<8$), so the injected signal is the only transit present ($336$ injections
across three hosts).

\section{Methods}
The pipeline fetches SPOC light curves (with a TESScut
full-frame-image fallback), detrends stellar variability with a Tukey biweight
filter, bins gap-aware to 10-minute cadence, and runs Transit Least Squares
\cite{hippke2019tls} with stellar priors from the TIC. From the TLS solution we
extract eight physical features --- period, transit depth (ppm), duration (hr),
model SNR, $R_p/R_\star$, and log/ratio transforms --- in units matched to the KOI
catalog, avoiding train/serve skew. A soft-voting RandomForest\,+\,XGBoost
ensemble is probability-calibrated (Platt scaling) on a held-out split; in Kepler
cross-validation it reaches ROC-AUC $0.964\pm0.006$ and a Brier score of $0.080$.
Five orthogonal physics checks --- signal significance (SDE $\geq 7$), odd/even
depth (Welch $t$-test with a $10\%$ effect-size guard), transit duration vs.\
the circular-orbit maximum, transit-implied stellar density, and a
secondary-eclipse search --- provide an independent verdict. A target ``passes
physics'' only if all five checks pass. Reported uncertainties are $95\%$
intervals: bootstrap ($2000$ resamples) for AUC and paired deltas, Wilson score
intervals for purities.

\section{Injection--recovery completeness}
Figure~\ref{fig:completeness} shows the fraction of injected transits recovered
(TLS finds the injected period at SDE $\geq 7$) across the period--radius grid,
and the classifier-recovery map (additionally labeled a planet); overall recovery
is $0.71$ (TLS) and $0.55$ (TLS\,+\,classifier), both scored at the deployed
operating point of \S\ref{sec:threshold}. Detection efficiency vs.\ injected SNR
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:effsnr}. Three features are notable and physically
correct: sub-$2\,R_\oplus$ planets fall below the recovery floor ($0.00$ at
$0.8\,R_\oplus$, $0.08$ at $1.5\,R_\oplus$); recovery declines with period as
fewer transits fall in a sector ($0.69$ at $2.4$\,d to $0.43$ at $12$\,d); and
the largest injected radii are recovered by TLS but preferentially dropped by the
classifier, whose recovery peaks near $9\,R_\oplus$ ($0.90$) and falls to $0.67$
at $13\,R_\oplus$ even though TLS finds $0.98$ of them. That last decline is the
learned planet/EB boundary: depths implying $R_p/R_\star \gtrsim 0.2$ look
eclipsing-binary-like. We note this suppression is much weaker than it appears
under a naive $0.5$ decision cut, where giant-radius recovery collapses to $0.12$
and the map's peak shifts misleadingly to $4$--$6\,R_\oplus$ --- an artifact of
the operating point rather than a property of the model.

\section{Cross-mission transfer}
\label{sec:transfer}
On the TESS benchmark the calibrated ensemble scores ROC-AUC
$0.72$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.67,0.76]$), far below its Kepler cross-validation AUC of
$0.964$, and its Brier score degrades from $0.08$ to $0.29$: the Kepler-calibrated
probabilities are miscalibrated on TESS (Figure~\ref{fig:tessroc}). The interval
excludes both chance ($0.5$) and the in-mission value, so the transfer drop is
large and robust. Class separation exists but is much weaker than in-mission: this
is what a headline same-mission AUC hides.

\paragraph{Comparison with simpler models.}
Figure~\ref{fig:baselines} compares, under an identical Kepler-train /
TESS-test protocol, an SDE/SNR threshold, logistic regression, a shallow tree, an
MLP, our ensemble, and a 1D-CNN on parametric folded views. The ensemble is the
strongest cross-mission (AUC $0.71$), ahead of the tree ($0.66$), logistic
($0.64$), the physical SNR cut ($0.64$), the CNN ($0.64$), and the MLP ($0.55$).
(We note that at a smaller $160$-target sample the SNR cut appeared to match the
ensemble; that apparent tie did not survive the larger benchmark --- a concrete
example of why sample size and confidence intervals matter here.)

\paragraph{Effect of additional sectors.}
Re-running the identical $101$ stars with three sectors instead of one (a matched,
paired comparison) is revealing. Period recovery rises by $+0.069$
($[0.000,0.139]$), the Brier score improves by $-0.040$ ($[-0.087,0.006]$), and
the mean probability on true planets climbs from $0.33$ to $0.45$ --- yet ranking
is unchanged, $\Delta\text{AUC}=+0.001$ ($[-0.068,0.067]$). The detection and
calibration gains are suggestive but not significant at this sample size; the null
effect on \emph{ranking} is the robust result. More data buys detection and
calibration, not cross-mission separability: the Kepler$\rightarrow$TESS gap is a
distribution-shift problem, not merely a signal-to-noise one. (Multi-sector
retrieval also failed for $\sim\!37\%$ of targets owing to intermittent archive
availability, a practical cost of the extra data.)

\paragraph{Mechanism of the transfer gap.}
The transfer gap is not a hand-wave: the \emph{separating direction} of the depth
and radius features inverts between the two populations. In the Kepler training
set deep transits skew toward eclipsing-binary false positives; in the TESS TOI
sample the deepest, largest-$R_p/R_\star$ signals are disproportionately the
\emph{confirmed} planets (planet vs.\ FP medians $4210$ vs.\ $1160$\,ppm and
$0.058$ vs.\ $0.031$; both Mann--Whitney $p<10^{-4}$). A model that learned
``deep\,$\Rightarrow$\,false positive'' on Kepler is therefore actively
mis-oriented on TESS --- explaining why more data cannot close the gap and why a
mission-agnostic SNR cut competes with it.

\section{The operating point is part of the transfer problem}
\label{sec:threshold}
A transferred model is usually reported by its ROC-AUC, a threshold-free
quantity. Deployment, however, requires choosing a threshold, and we find that
this choice --- not the model --- dominates the practical failure of cross-mission
transfer.

Because the Kepler-calibrated probabilities are compressed toward zero on TESS
(\S\ref{sec:transfer}), the conventional $p \geq 0.5$ cut is far too strict. On the $500$-target
benchmark it recovers only $28.4\%$ of true planets, discarding $179$ of $250$.
The Youden-optimal cut on the same scores is $p \geq 0.117$, which raises recall
to $0.724$ at a precision of $0.670$ --- $110$ additional real planets, at the
cost of $28 \rightarrow 89$ false alarms. The $F_1$ score improves from $0.407$
to $0.696$. Crucially, this is not threshold tuning on the evaluation set: under
$5$-fold cross-validation, with the threshold selected on the training fold and
applied to the held-out fold, the selected value is identical in every fold
($0.117$) and held-out recall is $0.728 \pm 0.033$.

The effect is concentrated in exactly the regions where the feature-direction
inversion bites. At $p \geq 0.5$ two subgroups have \emph{zero} recall: all $43$
planets with $P < 2$\,d, and all $19$ with depths above $20{,}000$\,ppm. These
are TESS's most characteristic detections --- short-period giants --- and the
Kepler prior ``deep and short $\Rightarrow$ eclipsing binary'' rejects every one.
Moving the threshold restores them only partially ($0.349$ and $0.316$
respectively; Table~\ref{tab:blindspots}), confirming that the operating point is
a large but incomplete remedy for a genuine orientation error.

\paragraph{Two model-level remedies that did not work.}
We tested whether the inversion could be repaired at the model level rather than
the threshold. First, dropping the mission-inverting features: retraining without
depth (and without $R_p/R_\star$) does \emph{not} improve transfer --- TESS AUC
moves from $0.703$ to $0.699$ and $0.674$ respectively, i.e.\ unchanged or worse.
The shift is distributed across the feature space, not carried by one or two
removable columns. Second, fusing the physics flags with the ML score: a logistic
combiner over the score and the five checks, evaluated out-of-fold, gives
$\Delta\text{AUC}=+0.005$ with a bootstrap interval of $[-0.015,+0.023]$ --- not
significant. Both negatives reinforce the paper's theme: under this
distribution shift, the recoverable gain lay in the decision rule, not in
feature selection or late fusion.

\section{ML $\times$ physics: agreement, and the limits of disagreement}
The ML probability and the physics verdict are independent judgements. Crossing
them yields four quadrants (Figure~\ref{fig:quad}, Table~\ref{tab:quad}).

\paragraph{Agreement is strongly predictive.}
When both judgements agree, the answer is trustworthy: both-say-planet is $79\%$
pure ($[0.68,0.87]$) and both-say-FP is $66\%$ pure in false positives
($[0.59,0.72]$). In prevalence-independent terms a planet is $3.7\times$ more
likely than a false positive to land in the both-agree-planet cell, and
$0.52\times$ as likely in the both-agree-FP cell. The physics verdict carries real
orthogonal signal on its own: it is significantly associated with the truth
(Fisher odds ratio $2.5$, $p<10^{-3}$; \S\ref{sec:validation}) and its five checks together reject
$54\%$ of the false positives that the learned score would rank as planet-like.

\paragraph{Disagreement is only weakly informative.}
It is tempting to read the off-diagonal cells as ``where the hidden planets are.''
The data do not support a strong version of that claim. The large ``ML-skeptical,
physics-pass'' quadrant ($n=217$) is $54\%$ planets ($[0.47,0.60]$) --- its
interval includes the $50\%$ base rate, so it is at best marginally enriched
($1.15\times$ lift). ``ML-optimistic, physics-fail'' is $55\%$ planets
($[0.38,0.72]$), also indistinguishable from chance. In an earlier $160$-target
sample the ML-skeptical cell looked cleanly enriched ($61\%$); that signal did not
hold up. The honest conclusion is that \emph{agreement} between the two verdicts is
what carries confidence, while \emph{disagreement} mostly marks genuinely
ambiguous objects rather than a reservoir of mislabeled planets.

\paragraph{Disagreement does not improve follow-up efficiency.}
If disagreement carried actionable information, a disagreement-aware ranking should
recover planets under a limited follow-up budget faster than probability alone.
Figure~\ref{fig:triage} plots true planets recovered against the fraction of
targets observed for four rankings. At $n=500$ the physics-informed orderings and
ML probability are statistically indistinguishable (gain-curve areas
$0.61$/$0.61$ vs.\ $0.61$; random $0.50$, oracle $0.75$). A modest advantage seen
at $n=160$ did not replicate. We report this as a null result: incorporating the
physics verdict into a follow-up ranking does not, on this benchmark, beat simply
trusting the calibrated probability.

\section{Ablations}
Leave-one-group-out feature ablation (retraining on Kepler, testing on TESS) shows
the shape features ($R_p/R_\star$, duration/period) carry the most transferable
signal ($\Delta\text{AUC}=-0.057$ when dropped), followed by SNR ($-0.035$);
dropping the depth features costs almost nothing ($-0.009$), consistent with depth
being the most mission-specific feature. A physics-check ablation on the TESS false
positives shows the five checks together flag $54\%$ of them, with the SDE
($32\%$) and odd/even ($14\%$) tests contributing most --- orthogonal rejection the
learned score does not supply. Full ablation tables accompany the released code.

\section{Validation}
\label{sec:validation}
Several checks confirm the results are real rather than pipeline artifacts
(Figure~\ref{fig:perm}). A permutation test --- shuffling the TESS labels
$5000\times$ --- collapses the AUC to a null centered at $0.50$ ($[0.45,0.55]$),
with the observed $0.72$ far outside it ($p=0$); the cross-mission signal is
genuine. Both verdicts are significantly associated with the truth (Fisher exact:
physics-pass odds ratio $2.5$, $p<10^{-3}$; ML prediction odds ratio $3.1$,
$p<10^{-3}$), so the quadrant structure is not noise. For all $350$
period-recovered targets the TLS period matches the independent ExoFOP catalog
period to within $3\%$ (median error $0.07\%$), confirming detections are real,
not spurious. Training (Kepler KIC/KOI) and test (TESS TIC) target sets are
disjoint by construction, precluding leakage.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
The central message is methodological: a transit-detection pipeline is best
reported as a \emph{characterized, validated decision system} --- a completeness
map, a calibration curve, a cross-mission number with a confidence interval, and
an explicit account of what does \emph{not} replicate --- rather than a single
in-mission score. Three concrete, robust findings support acting on this:
cross-mission transfer is severe ($0.96\!\rightarrow\!0.72$) with a diagnosable
mechanism (feature-direction inversion); the deployed \emph{operating point}
carries more practical weight than the model choice, with the conventional $0.5$
cut discarding $72\%$ of real planets and blinding the pipeline entirely to
short-period and deep transits; and independent physics vetting supplies real
orthogonal false-positive rejection ($54\%$) that the learned score alone does not.
The threshold result generalizes beyond this pipeline: any calibrated classifier
carried across a domain shift inherits a source-calibrated decision rule, and
reporting only a threshold-free AUC hides the resulting deployment failure
entirely. We would encourage cross-mission studies to report a
recall-at-operating-point alongside AUC.
Where two independent verdicts \emph{agree}, confidence is high; where they
disagree, the object is genuinely ambiguous --- we found no evidence that
disagreement hides a recoverable population of planets, and we report that
negative honestly.

Limitations: the benchmark uses single-sector light curves for tractability (a
three-sector experiment improves detection but not ranking); the $500$-target size
still yields intervals of $\pm0.05$ on the AUC; the 1D-CNN uses parametric folded
views rather than raw pixels, leaving a full AstroNet-style detector to future
work; injection hosts are screened for quietness though real correlated noise
varies across the sky; and the class-balanced benchmark matches the dispositioned-TOI
base rate ($\approx0.5$) but overstates purity for a rarer blind-search population,
for which we report Bayes-adjusted values.
