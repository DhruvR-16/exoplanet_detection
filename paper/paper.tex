\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Most machine-learning studies of transiting-exoplanet detection report a single
in-mission accuracy or AUC. We argue this is the wrong figure of merit and
present instead a \emph{characterized decision system}: an interpretable,
physics-informed pipeline (Transit Least Squares $\rightarrow$ 8 physical
features $\rightarrow$ a calibrated RandomForest\,+\,XGBoost ensemble $\rightarrow$
five orthogonal physics-vetting checks) whose behavior we map rather than
summarize. Three results follow. (i) \textbf{Injection--recovery}: injecting
Mandel--Agol transits into real, screened-quiet TESS light curves, we measure
detection completeness as a function of period, planet radius and SNR, exposing
the true sensitivity floor. (ii) \textbf{Cross-mission transfer}: a classifier
that scores ROC-AUC $0.96$ in Kepler cross-validation falls to $0.68$ on a
labeled TESS test set, and --- strikingly --- \emph{no learned model beats a
physical SNR cut} cross-mission. (iii) \textbf{Disagreement as a signal}: because
the ML score and the physics verdict are independent, their disagreement is
informative --- the ``ML-skeptical, physics-pass'' quadrant is majority true
planets (over half of all planets in the set), i.e.\ the physics rescues planets
the Kepler-trained model rejects on TESS. We propose ML--physics disagreement as an actionable triage
ranking for scarce follow-up resources. All figures and tables regenerate from
raw archives with a single command.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The transiting-exoplanet literature has converged on supervised classifiers that
map light curves or hand-built features to a planet/false-positive label, and
reports their success as an accuracy or ROC-AUC on a held-out set drawn from the
\emph{same} mission \citep{shallue2018,exominer2021}. Such a number answers
``how separable is my test set?'' but not the questions a survey actually asks:
\emph{what can this pipeline detect}, \emph{does it work on data it was not
trained on}, and \emph{where should a human look?} Recent work adds probability
calibration and multimodal inputs \citep{exonet2026} but still frames the output
as a single vetting score.

We take a different stance: the output of a detection pipeline should be a
\emph{characterized decision}, and the most useful information is often where two
independent judgements \emph{disagree}. Our pipeline emits two such judgements
--- a calibrated ML probability and an interpretable five-check physics verdict
--- and we show their disagreement is where the astrophysically interesting cases
live. Our contributions are: (1) an injection--recovery completeness map for a
feature-based classical ensemble on real TESS data; (2) an honest quantification
of Kepler$\rightarrow$TESS transfer, attributing the gap to detection rather than
vetting; (3) the ML--physics disagreement framework as a triage signal; and (4) a
fully reproducible, interpretable baseline others can extend.

\section{Data}
\textbf{Training (Kepler).} We train on the NASA Exoplanet Archive KOI cumulative
catalog, using the definitive dispositions \textsc{confirmed} (positive) and
\textsc{false positive} (negative) and excluding \textsc{candidate}s
($N=7325$; 2745 planets, 4580 false positives).

\textbf{Test (TESS).} We build a labeled TESS benchmark from ExoFOP TFOPWG
dispositions --- \textsc{cp}/\textsc{kp} as planets, \textsc{fp}/\textsc{fa} as
false positives --- and run the full pipeline on a class-balanced sample of TIC
targets. This is the only place true TESS labels enter, and it measures real
deployment performance.

\textbf{Injection corpus.} For completeness testing we inject synthetic transits
into real TESS light curves screened to be transit-free (pre-injection
TLS\,SDE $<8$), so the injected signal is the only transit present.

\section{Methods}
The pipeline (\texttt{pipeline.py}) fetches SPOC light curves (with a TESScut
full-frame-image fallback), detrends stellar variability with a Tukey biweight
filter, bins gap-aware to 10-minute cadence, and runs Transit Least Squares
\citep{hippke2019tls} with stellar priors from the TIC. From the TLS solution we
extract eight physical features (period, depth, duration, model SNR, $R_p/R_\star$,
and log/ratio transforms) in units matched to the KOI catalog, avoiding
train/serve skew. A soft-voting RandomForest\,+\,XGBoost ensemble is
probability-calibrated on a held-out split. Five orthogonal physics checks ---
signal significance (SDE), odd/even depth (Welch $t$-test with an effect-size
guard), transit-duration vs.\ circular-orbit maximum, transit-implied stellar
density, and a secondary-eclipse search --- provide an independent verdict.

\section{Cross-mission transfer}
On the TESS benchmark ($N=160$, class-balanced) the calibrated ensemble scores
ROC-AUC $0.68$, far below its Kepler cross-validation AUC of $0.964$
(Figure~\ref{fig:tessroc}), and its Brier score degrades from $0.08$ to $0.31$
--- the Kepler-calibrated probabilities are miscalibrated on TESS. Crucially,
Figure~\ref{fig:baselines} shows \emph{no} learned model (logistic regression,
tree, MLP, our ensemble, or a 1D-CNN) beats a simple physical SNR threshold
(AUC $0.68$) cross-mission: the learned feature combinations overfit
Kepler-specific structure that does not transfer. Decomposing the gap, TLS
recovers the true period in only $\sim\!67\%$ of single-sector light curves;
\emph{conditioned on correct detection} class separation improves modestly. The
transfer gap is therefore substantially a detection-domain-shift effect --- a
distinction invisible to a single-number report.

\section{Disagreement as a triage signal}
The ML probability and the physics verdict are independent judgements. Crossing
them yields four quadrants (Figure~\ref{fig:quad}). Agreement is the common,
low-information case; \emph{disagreement} concentrates the interesting objects.
Empirically the ``ML-skeptical, physics-pass'' quadrant is majority true planets
--- the Kepler-trained model, seeing weaker TESS signals, rejects planets that the
mission-agnostic physics correctly passes --- while ``ML-optimistic,
physics-fail'' flags false positives the score alone would admit. We therefore
propose ranking follow-up by disagreement rather than by probability alone.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
Our benchmark uses single-sector TESS light curves for tractability, which
lower-bounds detection performance; multi-sector stitching would raise
completeness. The 1D-CNN is trained on parametric folded views rather than raw
pixels; a full AstroNet-style detector is future work. Injection hosts are
screened for quietness but real correlated noise varies across the sky. None of
these change the central message: a detection pipeline is best reported as a
characterized decision system, and the disagreement between a learned score and
independent physics is a resource worth spending telescope time on.
